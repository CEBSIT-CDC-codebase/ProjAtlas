Q: How many LHA neurons were recorded during the single-unit recording across 7 mice walking on a linear track?
A: A total of 156 neurons were recorded from the 7 mice.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: A total of 156 neurons were recorded from 7 mice.

Q: Among the 37 speed-correlated LHA neurons, how many were classified as putative glutamatergic?
A: Twenty-four out of the 37 neurons exhibited characteristics of putative glutamatergic neurons.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Out of these 37 neurons, we found that 24 exhibited characteristics of putative glutamatergic neurons, 3 exhibited characteristics of GABAergic neurons, and the remaining 10 were indistinguishable.

Q: What happens when LHA Vglut2+ neurons are optogenetically stimulated?
A: The mice produce robust locomotion in a frequency- and light intensity-dependent manner.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Further experiments on optogenetic stimulation of Vglut2-ChR2 expressing neurons in 10 hypothalamic nuclei in free-moving mice (Figures S1C-S1E), we found that only the activation of LHA $Vglut2^{+}$ neurons resulted in robust locomotion of the mice, in a frequency- and light intensity-dependent manner (Figures 1F-H).

Q: Does optogenetic stimulation of LHA spinal cord-projecting neurons directly initiate locomotion?
A: No, optogenetic activation of these LHA neurons does not elicit locomotor behaviors.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We found that optogenetic stimulation of these spinal cord-projecting LHA neurons did not elicit locomotor behaviors (Figures 2I-K).

Q: Which downstream brain regions of LHA projections can initiate locomotion when optogenetically activated?
A: The pontine oral part (PnO) and the medial septum and diagonal band (MSDB).
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Notably, activation of LHA neuron projection to the PnO or MSDB could initiate locomotion, whereas MSDB activation led to high-speed locomotion, in contrast to the more moderate responses elicited by PnO activation (Figures 2I-2K).

Q: What proportion of LHA neurons in the long-distance projecting cluster (cluster 4) directly extend axons to the spinal cord?
A: Nearly half (111 out of 233) of the neurons in cluster 4 send direct projections to the spinal cord.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Among cluster 4 neurons, nearly half (111 out of 233) of the neurons had direct projections to the spinal cord (Figure 2A).

Q: Where are the cell bodies of spinal-projecting excitatory PnO neurons predominantly located?
A: Their somata are predominantly located in the lateral part of the PnO.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Through retrogradely labeling from the lumbar spinal cord, we found somata of spinal-projecting PnO excitatory neurons were mainly located in the lateral part of PnO (Figure 3I and 3J).

Q: What fraction of LHA spinal cord-projecting neurons express Orexin?
A: Approximately 48.99% of the LHA neurons that project to the spinal cord express Orexin.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Interestingly, approximately $48.99\%$ of neurons that project to the spinal cord express Orexin, while subtypes projecting to the PnO and MSDB comprise a heterogeneous population of peptidergic neurons.

Q: What is the behavioral consequence of selectively depleting PnO-projecting LHA neuron terminals?
A: Depletion of PnO-projecting LHA neurons impairs motivation-related locomotion, as shown by reduced total distance traveled and fewer entries into the center of an open field.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Through injecting retrogradely transported AAVs carrying Cre into the PnO while expressing Cre-dependent DTR in the LHA of wild-type mice, we found that depletion of PnO-projecting rather than MSDB-projecting LHA neuron terminals impaired motivation-related locomotion, as indicated by the reduced total distance and entries to the center compared to the control group (Figures 3D and 3E).

Q: Are LHA glutamatergic neurons required for basic locomotor functions?
A: No, ablation of LHA glutamatergic neurons spares basic locomotion such as hindlimb protraction and open field activity, but these neurons are essential for motivated locomotor activity.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We found that glutamatergic neurons in lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) are essential for regulating motivated locomotor activity.
  - Evidence 2: Following diphtheria toxin (DT) administration, no significant changes were observed in basic locomotor functions such as hindlimb protraction, retraction, iliac crest height, or overall activity in an open field setting (Figures 1N-1R), suggesting that the ablation of LHA glutamatergic neurons did not impact basic locomotion.

Q: What behavioral effects are induced by optogenetic stimulation of MSDB-projecting LHA neurons?
A: It triggers high-speed locomotion and produces anxiety-like behaviors, manifesting as significantly reduced time in the center of an open field and in the open arms of an elevated plus-maze.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Notably, activation of LHA neuron projection to the PnO or MSDB could initiate locomotion, whereas MSDB activation led to high-speed locomotion, in contrast to the more moderate responses elicited by PnO activation (Figures 2I-2K).
  - Evidence 2: Further open field test and elevated plus-maze test for the stress response of the mice indicated that stimulation of MSDB-projecting LHA neurons resulted in a significant reduction in the time at the center during the open field test and in the time in the open arms during the elevated plus-maze test.

Q: What experimental evidence supports the specific involvement of the LHA-PnO pathway in motivated locomotion?
A: Optogenetic activation of LHA-PnO projections initiates locomotion, and selective ablation of PnO-projecting LHA terminals impairs motivation-driven locomotion.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Notably, activation of LHA neuron projection to the PnO or MSDB could initiate locomotion, whereas MSDB activation led to high-speed locomotion, in contrast to the more moderate responses elicited by PnO activation (Figures 2I-2K).
  - Evidence 2: Through injecting retrogradely transported AAVs carrying Cre into the PnO while expressing Cre-dependent DTR in the LHA of wild-type mice, we found that depletion of PnO-projecting rather than MSDB-projecting LHA neuron terminals impaired motivation-related locomotion, as indicated by the reduced total distance and entries to the center compared to the control group (Figures 3D and 3E).

Q: Does the LHA use direct spinal cord projections to drive locomotion?
A: No, direct optogenetic stimulation of LHA spinal cord-projecting neurons does not elicit locomotion; instead, the LHA indirectly activates the spinal cord through a relay in the PnO.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We found that optogenetic stimulation of these spinal cord-projecting LHA neurons did not elicit locomotor behaviors (Figures 2I-K).
  - Evidence 2: Together, these results indicate that LHA indirectly activate spinal cord neurons through a relay station of PnO to induce locomotion behavior.

Q: Can both excitatory and inhibitory LHA neurons initiate locomotion when optogenetically stimulated?
A: No, only stimulation of excitatory Vglut2+ LHA neurons elicits robust locomotion, while stimulation of inhibitory Vgat+ LHA neurons fails to induce locomotion.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Further experiments on optogenetic stimulation of Vglut2-ChR2 expressing neurons in 10 hypothalamic nuclei in free-moving mice (Figures S1C-S1E), we found that only the activation of LHA $Vglut2^{+}$ neurons resulted in robust locomotion of the mice, in a frequency- and light intensity-dependent manner (Figures 1F-H).
  - Evidence 2: Meanwhile, in open field test, optical stimulation of LHA $Vgat^{+}$ cannot induce locomotion in freely-moving mice (Figure S1I).

Q: How do LHA glutamatergic neurons relay locomotor commands down to the spinal cord?
A: They send projections to the PnO, which is rich in reticulospinal neurons that directly innervate the spinal cord; stimulating either the LHA-PnO connection or the PnO-spinal cord pathway alone can initiate locomotion.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Stimulation of the cell bodies of LHA neurons projecting to PnO or MSDB successfully induced locomotion in freely moving mice (Figures 2L-2N, S2D and S2E).
  - Evidence 2: Our anterograde tracing experiment showed that PnO comprised a majority of pontine reticulospinal neurons which directly projected to the spinal cord (Figure 3G and 3H).

Q: What evidence links LHA glutamatergic neuron activity to food-seeking locomotion?
A: Calcium signals in LHA excitatory neurons increase during food-seeking and locomotion onset, and selective ablation of these neurons reduces food-seeking locomotor activity without affecting appetite.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We observed an increase of $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ signals of excitatory neurons that was time locked to the onset of locomotion, with elevated activity during food seeking behavior when approaching the food.
  - Evidence 2: However, in the open field chamber with food pellets located in the center, the locomotor activity was reduced and the mice were less likely to enter the center area, despite the absence of any impact on their appetite measured by food pellet consumption.

Q: How are PRV-labeled hypothalamic neurons distributed within the LHA?
A: The LHA contains the highest proportion (36.6%) of labeled hypothalamic neurons, and within the LHA, the highest density is found in the caudal region.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Further analysis showed that labeled neurons were widely distributed across the hypothalamus, with the highest density (36.6 % of the total) in the lateral hypothalamus area (LHA) (Figures 1B-D and S1A-B).
  - Evidence 2: Further kernel density estimation (KDE) of trans-synaptically retrogradely labeled neurons indicated that the highest density of labeled neurons was located in the caudal region of LHA (cLHA, Figure 1E), which contains both excitatory and inhibitory neurons<sup>32</sup>.

Q: What motor deficit appears in mice 3 days after a thoracic lateral hemisection at T10?
A: Mice lose the ability to step with the ipsilateral hindlimb, although the contralateral hindlimb's muscle function remains intact.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "3 days post injury, mice displayed a complete loss of stepping ability in the ipsilateral hindlimb, though muscle function in the contralateral hindlimb remained intact."

Q: What spontaneous locomotor recovery is observed in the chronic phase after incomplete spinal cord injury?
A: All animals show significant recovery, characterized by full-body weight-bearing plantar stepping.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "In contrast, in the chronic phase post-SCI, we observed significant locomotor recovery in all subjects, characterized by full-body weight-bearing plantar stepping (Figures S4A and S4B)."

Q: How does PRV labeling from hindlimb muscle change in the brain at the early stage after SCI?
A: At 1 week post-injury, there is a marked reduction in neuronal labeling across the brain compared to intact mice, reflecting disrupted descending pathways to spinal locomotor circuits.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Mice injected with PRV into GS/TA muscle at the early stage exhibited a marked reduction in neuronal labeling across the brain compared to intact mice, indicating disrupted descending pathways to the locomotor circuits below the lesion."

Q: What change occurs in LHA neuronal labeling 8 weeks after spinal cord injury?
A: Mice show a significant increase of labeled neurons in the LHA, suggesting that the brain undergoes reorganization or enhanced neuronal connections over time.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "In contrast, mice injected with the virus 8 weeks post-injury demonstrated a significant increase of labeled neurons in LHA, suggesting substantial brain reorganization or enhancement of neuronal connections over time."

Q: Does electrical stimulation of the lateral hypothalamus induce hindlimb muscle contraction in intact mice?
A: Yes, it induces TA muscle contraction as measured by electromyography.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "In intact mice, electrical stimulation of the LHA induced TA muscle contraction, as recorded by electromyography."

Q: Can electrical LHA stimulation trigger TA muscle activation at 1 week after an incomplete SCI?

Q: What is the consequence of depleting LHA Vglut2+ neurons on functional recovery after SCI?
A: Depleted mice exhibit impaired recovery with pronounced paw dragging and impaired protraction of the denervated hindlimb.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "We found that mice with depletion of LHA $Vglut2^{+}$ neurons exhibited impaired recovery of locomotor function characterized by pronounced paw dragging and impaired protraction of the denervated hindlimb (Figures 4D-F)."

Q: How does genetic ablation of PnO-projecting LHA neurons affect locomotor recovery after incomplete SCI?
A: It significantly impairs functional recovery, causing pronounced paw dragging and impaired hindlimb protraction during over-ground locomotion.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Genetic ablation of PnO-projecting LHA neurons significantly impaired functional recovery during over-ground locomotion evidenced by pronounced paw dragging and impaired protraction of the denervated hindlimb (Figures 4H-J)."

Q: Does optogenetic stimulation of LHA neurons improve hindlimb locomotion in mice after SCI?
A: Yes, all mice demonstrated significantly improved hindlimb locomotion during light stimulation at tested post-injury time points.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "All tested mice demonstrated significantly improved hindlimb locomotion during light stimulation."

Q: Does activating direct spinal cord-projecting LHA neurons produce locomotion?
A: No, activation of these neurons does not induce locomotion.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "However, activation of these direct spinal cord-projecting neurons in the LHA did not induce locomotion."

Q: Why is the caudal part of the LHA targeted for DBS to improve locomotion without causing anxiety?
A: The cell bodies of LHA neurons that project to the PnO (which promotes locomotion) reside mainly in the caudal LHA, while rostral LHA neurons project to the MSDB, a pathway that when stimulated induces anxiety-like behaviors detrimental to recovery.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Based on the somatic distribution of LHA neurons projecting to the $\mathrm{PnO}$ and MSDB, we identified that the cell bodies of LHA neurons projecting to the $\mathrm{PnO}$ are primarily located in the caudal region of the LHA (Figure 3L), whereas those projecting to the MSDB are predominantly situated in the rostral region."
  - Evidence 2: "Our results indicated that stimulation of the LHA-MSDB pathway could induce significant anxiety-like behaviors (Figures 3H and 3I) that were detrimental to recovery."

Q: What evidence demonstrates that LHA undergoes functional reorganization that contributes to spontaneous recovery after SCI?
A: Acutely after injury, PRV tracing shows diminished brain connectivity, but at 8 weeks, LHA electrical stimulation once again produces TA muscle activation, indicating a re-establishment of functional connections.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Mice injected with PRV into GS/TA muscle at the early stage exhibited a marked reduction in neuronal labeling across the brain compared to intact mice, indicating disrupted descending pathways to the locomotor circuits below the lesion."
  - Evidence 2: "However, stimulation of the same LHA coordinates successfully restored the ability to trigger TA muscle activation 8 weeks post-SCI (Figure 4B)."

Q: How does the motivational drive encoded by PnO-projecting LHA neurons influence recovery after incomplete SCI?
A: The motivational locomotion mediated by these neurons is essential for natural repair; their ablation impairs both food-seeking motivation and locomotor recovery, and similarly, depletion of LHA Vglut2+ neurons reduces motivation and recovery.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "These findings suggest that motivational locomotion encoded by PnO-projecting LHA neurons was essential for natural repair after incomplete SCI."
  - Evidence 2: "The impaired locomotor recovery was associated with decreased motivation for food seeking indicated by decreased total distance and entries to the center area (Figures 4G and S4D)."

Q: What is the time course of LHA-DBS effectiveness in a double hemisection SCI model, and what cellular change accompanies the delayed response?
A: No hindlimb response occurs at 1 week post-injury, but by 4 weeks walking improves, and at later stages there is an increase in cFos/Zfhx3 double-positive neurons in the inter-lesion spinal cord, suggesting recruitment of relay pathways.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Initially, no hindlimb responses were observed with LHA-DBS at 1 week post-injury."
  - Evidence 2: "Target DBS at cLHA led to a significant increase in cFos-positive neurons, as well as c-Fos and Zfhx3 double-positive neurons, in the inter-lesion spinal cord at 4- and 10-weeks post-injury, compared to the early stage following SCI (Figures S5A-S5C)."

Q: How does brain-controlled LHA-DBS avoid the stress responses associated with conventional LHA-DBS?
A: Stochastic LHA-DBS triggers elevated stress levels, but a BCI-DBS system that delivers stimulation upon decoding locomotor intention from motor cortex activity does not provoke stress-like responses.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Previous findings have demonstrated that the delivery of DBS triggers stress responses in animals $^{27}$ and elevated stress levels were also observed under the condition of stochastic LHA-DBS despite stimulating specific locus at cLHA."
  - Evidence 2: "Under conditions of brain-controlled electrical stimulation, LHA-DBS did not provoke stress-like responses in either healthy or injured mice (Figures 6C-6F)."

Q: How does the BCI-DBS system support long-term locomotor recovery after severe SCI?
A: It decodes the intention to walk from MUA in the motor cortex to time LHA stimulation, and chronic training with this closed-loop system restores hindlimb stepping and increases BMS scores.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "The intention of locomotion can be decoded from the multi-unit activity (MUA) in the motor cortex's hindlimb region (M1HL) $^{27}$."
  - Evidence 2: "In contrast to the paralyzed control group, long-term BCI-DBS training resulted in significant functional recovery, including restored hindlimb stepping ability and increased BMS scores."

Q: What is the proposed mechanism by which LHA-DBS promotes locomotor recovery, and what histological evidence supports it?
A: LHA-DBS is thought to activate Zfhx3-positive long-distance projecting neurons; both acute DBS and long-term BCI-DBS training increase cFos/Zfhx3 double-positive neurons in the inter-lesion spinal cord.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Consequently, the mechanisms underlying LHA-DBS are likely dependent on the activation of Zfhx3 neurons, which are predominantly found among long-distance projecting neurons $^{45}$."
  - Evidence 2: "Furthermore, long-term BCI-DBS training led to a significant increase in cfos-positive and cfos/Zfhx3 double-positive neurons in the inter-lesion spinal cord (Figures S6A-S6C)."

Q: What makes the LHA-PnO pathway more suitable for therapeutic DBS than the LHA-MSDB pathway?
A: Stimulating the LHA-PnO circuit enhances locomotion without inducing anxiety or stress, whereas activation of the LHA-MSDB pathway induces anxiety-like behaviors that impede recovery.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "Our results indicated that stimulation of the LHA-MSDB pathway could induce significant anxiety-like behaviors (Figures 3H and 3I) that were detrimental to recovery."
  - Evidence 2: "Importantly, locomotion was observed without the induction of anxiety or stress-like behaviors, indicating that the LHA-PnO circuit can selectively enhance motor functions associated with motivation without triggering negative emotional states."
