Q: How many transcriptome-defined cortical cell types does the macaque cortex atlas include?
A: The atlas includes 264 transcriptome-defined cortical cell types.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Using large-scale single-nucleus RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomic analysis of 143 macaque cortical regions, we obtained a comprehensive atlas of 264 transcriptome-defined cortical cell types and mapped their spatial distribution across the entire cortex.

Q: What are the five prominent marker genes used to annotate GABAergic neuron subclasses?
A: The five prominent marker genes are LAMP5, VIP, RELN, parvalbumin (PV), and somatostatin (SST).
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: GABAergic neuron subclasses were annotated by 5 prominent marker genes: lysosome-associated membrane protein 5 (LAMP5), vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), reelin (RELN), parvalbumin (PV), and somatostatin (SST).

Q: Which cortical region had the highest cell density?
A: The primary visual cortex (V1) had the highest density, at approximately 1500 cells per µm².
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Further analysis of our stereo-seq cell-type maps showed marked cell density differences among various cortical regions, with the highest density in V1 $( \sim 1 , 5 0 0 \ c e | | \mathsf { s } / \mu \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } )$ ) and the lowest density in the entorhinal cortex (Elc; 200 cells $\cdot \mathrm { \mu m } ^ { 2 } )$ ) (Figure S3A).

Q: What was the average number of genes detected per cell in the stereo-seq analysis?
A: An average of 458 genes per cell were detected.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Using this method, we obtained 266,310 segmented cortical cells per section with an average of 819 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and 458 genes per cell (Figure 2C; Table S3).

Q: What are the three major cell classes at level 1 of the transcriptomic taxonomy?
A: The three major cell classes are glutamatergic, GABAergic, and non-neuronal cells.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Level 1 represents three major cell classes: glutamatergic (969,794), GABAergic (268,097) and non-neuronal (97,298) cells.

Q: How many cortical cells were mapped in the spatial transcriptome map?
A: 42,076,954 cortical cells were mapped.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Using this method, we identified cell types for 42,076,954 cortical cells in the spatial transcriptome map.

Q: How many coronal sections at what spacing were obtained from monkey #1 for stereo-seq?
A: A total of 119 sections at 500-µm spacing were obtained from monkey #1.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: In monkey #1, we obtained a total of 119 sections at $5 0 0 - \mu \mathrm { m }$ spacing covering the entire cortex (Table S3), together with two 10- $\mu \mathsf { m }$ sections adjacent to each stereo-seq section for immunostaining and several adjacent $5 0 - \mu \mathrm { m }$ thick sections for regional microdissection and snRNA-seq analysis (see above, Figure 1A).

Q: What are the six non-neuronal subclasses identified in the macaque cortex?
A: The six non-neuronal subclasses are astrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells, oligodendrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, and vascular leptomeningeal cells.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: The 6 non-neuronal subclasses included astrocytes (ASCs), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), oligodendrocytes (OLGs), microglia (MG), endothelial cells (ECs), and vascular leptomeningeal cells (VLMCs), each annotated by their specific marker genes (Figure S1K).

Q: In which cortical layers are RELN subclass GABAergic neurons predominantly located?
A: RELN subclass GABAergic neurons are predominantly located in layers 1 and 2.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We next examined the layer and regional specificities of GABAergic cells and found that RELN subclass was predominantly located in L1/2, whereas LAMP5, VIP, and VIP_RELN subclasses were enriched in L2/3/4.

Q: What primate-specific cell type enrichment was found through cross-species comparison?
A: Primate-specific cell types were found to be enriched in layer 4, with their marker genes expressed in a region-dependent manner.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Cross-species comparison of transcriptomic data from human, macaque, and mouse cortices further revealed primate-specific cell types that are enriched in layer 4, with their marker genes expressed in a region-dependent manner.

Q: What is the total number of cells used for snRNA-seq, and how many of those came from monkey #1?
A: A total of 1,493,240 cells were used for snRNA-seq, of which 646,393 cells were obtained from monkey #1.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: A total of 1,493,240 cells from 143 regions of the entire cerebral cortex of two monkeys were used for snRNA-seq analysis and cell-type classification.
  - Evidence 2: In monkey #1, 646,393 cells were obtained from $5 0 - \mu \mathrm { m }$ coronal sections adjacent to those used for stereo-seq.

Q: What section thickness was used for stereo-seq, and what was the average number of segmented cells per section?
A: Stereo-seq used 10-µm coronal sections, and an average of 266,310 cells were segmented per section.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Stereo-seq analysis was performed on $1 0 \mathrm { - } \mu \mathrm { m }$ coronal sections from the freshly isolated left hemisphere of three adult male cynomolgus monkeys.
  - Evidence 2: Using this method, we obtained 266,310 segmented cortical cells per section with an average of 819 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and 458 genes per cell (Figure 2C; Table S3).

Q: How many cell types were identified, and what analysis showed that cortical lobes can be distinguished by cell-type composition?
A: 264 cell types were identified, and unsupervised clustering analysis revealed that 9 cortical lobes could be distinguished by their cell-type compositions.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: This led to the identification of 264 cell clusters for 143 cortical regions, which were grouped into 9 conventional lobes (prefrontal, frontal, occipital, temporal, parietal, auditory, somatosensory, cingulate, and insular; Figure 1B; Table S1).
  - Evidence 2: Unsupervised clustering analysis revealed that 9 major cortical lobes could be distinguished by their cell-type compositions based on the stereo-seq map (Figure 2G) and snRNA-seq data (Figure S2K).

Q: What are the five prominent GABAergic subclass marker genes, and what two SST cell groups were identified with complementary regional abundance?
A: The five markers are LAMP5, VIP, RELN, PV, and SST. SST cells formed group 1 enriched in prefrontal and cingulate cortices and group 2 enriched in occipital and parietal cortices.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: GABAergic neuron subclasses were annotated by 5 prominent marker genes: lysosome-associated membrane protein 5 (LAMP5), vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), reelin (RELN), parvalbumin (PV), and somatostatin (SST).
  - Evidence 2: We found two groups of SST cell types that showed ‘‘complementary abundance’’ in the macaque cortex: group 1 exhibited high density in the prefrontal and cingulate cortices, whereas group 2 mainly populated in the occipital and parietal cortices (Figure 5C).

Q: What spatial transcriptome method was employed, and what physical factors enabled single-cell resolution?
A: The stereo-seq method was employed; the 10-µm section thickness combined with preservation procedures that reduced mRNA diffusion enabled single-cell resolution.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: In this study, we employed a newly developed large-field-ofview spatial transcriptome method, termed ‘‘stereo-seq,28’ and developed a method for preparing centimeter-scale thin sections of the monkey brain.
  - Evidence 2: The $1 0 \mathrm { - } \mu \mathrm { m }$ section thickness, together with section preservation procedures that reduced mRNA diffusion, facilitated spatial transcriptome analysis at the single-cell resolution.

Q: What is the cell density in V1, and what gene is characteristic of V1-specific glutamatergic cell types?
A: V1 has a density of approximately 1500 cells per µm², and V1-specific cell types highly express NTNG1.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Further analysis of our stereo-seq cell-type maps showed marked cell density differences among various cortical regions, with the highest density in V1 $( \sim 1 , 5 0 0 \ c e | | \mathsf { s } / \mu \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } )$ ) and the lowest density in the entorhinal cortex (Elc; 200 cells $\cdot \mathrm { \mu m } ^ { 2 } )$ ) (Figure S3A).
  - Evidence 2: V1-specific cell types were characterized by a high expression of NTNG1, which is known to guide axon growth during neuronal development, and its polymorphism was associated with schizophrenia.

Q: How many monkeys provided brain tissue for snRNA-seq, and how many for stereo-seq?
A: Two monkeys were used for snRNA-seq, and three monkeys for stereo-seq.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: For snRNA-seq analysis, we used cortical tissues from two cynomolgus monkey brains.
  - Evidence 2: Stereo-seq analysis was performed on $1 0 \mathrm { - } \mu \mathrm { m }$ coronal sections from the freshly isolated left hemisphere of three adult male cynomolgus monkeys.

Q: What are the three major cell classes in the macaque cortex taxonomy, and how many glutamatergic cell types are highly localized to specific cortical regions?
A: The three major cell classes are glutamatergic, GABAergic, and non-neuronal cells. Nine glutamatergic cell types are highly localized to specific cortical regions.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Level 1 represents three major cell classes: glutamatergic (969,794), GABAergic (268,097) and non-neuronal (97,298) cells.
  - Evidence 2: We further zoomed in to the regional distribution of each glutamatergic cell type (Table S5) and found that nine glutamatergic cell types were highly localized to specific cortical regions (Figures 4C, 4D, and S4A).

Q: What is the function of chandelier cells in the cortex?
A: Chandelier cells innervate the axon initial segments of pyramidal neurons and can veto neuronal firing.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Chandelier cells innervate the axon initial segments (AISs) of pyramid neurons and could veto neuronal firing.

Q: Which chandelier cell type is most abundant in layer 2, and where are the other two types most abundant?
A: CHC.3 is the most abundant in layer 2, whereas CHC.1 and CHC.2 are most abundant in layer 4.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Among the three chandelier cell types, CHC.3 was the most abundant in L2, whereas CHC.1 and CHC.2 were dispersed among all layers and the most abundant in L4 (Figure 5F).

Q: In which cortical areas is the PV/(PV+SST) cell density ratio the highest?
A: The ratio is highest in motor cortices (F1–F3, F6, and F7) and the entorhinal area.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We therefore examined L2/3 PV/$( \mathsf { P V } + \mathsf { S S T } )$ cell density ratios and found that $^ { \mathrm { { 3 } } } \mathsf { V } / ( \mathsf { P } \mathsf { V } + \mathsf { S } \mathsf { S } \mathsf { T } )$ ) density ratio was the highest in motor cortices (F1–F3, F6, and F7) and the entorhinal area and the lowest in the insular and auditory cortices (Figure 5I).

Q: Does the PV and SST density ratio in macaque show a significant correlation with hierarchical levels in sensory systems?
A: No, it does not show any significant correlation with hierarchical levels in either the visual or somatosensory system.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: However, PV and SST density ratio in macaque did not show any significant correlation with the hierarchical levels in either sensory system (Figure S5L).

Q: What did principal component analysis reveal about PV and SST cell-type compositions and hierarchy?
A: The first principal component exhibited a positive correlation with the hierarchical levels of both the visual and somatosensory systems.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: The first principal component (PC1) of various regions exhibited a positive correlation with the hierarchical levels of both sensory systems (Figure 5J), suggesting that the PV and SST interneurons with distinct soma- and dendritic-targeting properties are tightly regulated to support the functions of various cortical regions.

Q: Which glutamatergic receptor families are expressed at higher levels in group 2 ASCs?
A: AMPA, Kainate, NMDA, and Delta receptor families are all expressed at higher levels in group 2 ASCs.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Further analysis revealed that the glutamatergic receptor genes, including AMPA, Kainate, NMDA, and Delta families, were all expressed at a higher level in the group 2 ASCs (Figure 6D).

Q: How many primate-specific glutamatergic L4 cell types were found in macaque and human but not in mouse?
A: Six such cell types were identified.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Remarkably, a pairwise comparison of the transcriptome profiles of cell types uncovered six glutamatergic L4 cell types that were present in the PFC, F1, and V1 regions of macaques and humans but not in mice (Figures 7A–7C, marked in red).

Q: In macaque cortical regions with a clear layer 4, where is FOXP2 expression highest?
A: FOXP2 expression is highest in layer 4 for most cortical regions.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We further quantified FOXP2 expression in each cortical layer of all regions of the macaque cortex and found that this gene showed the highest expression level in L4 for most cortical regions (Figure 7H).

Q: What is the average number of genes captured per cell by the spatial transcriptome method used in the study?
A: Approximately 500 genes per cell were captured.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: The average number of genes captured per cell (~500) by the present method was higher than that found by using previously reported methods, although it remained much lower than that obtained by conventional snRNA-seq method.

Q: How does the regional distribution of non-neuronal cell types correlate with cortical hierarchy?
A: The regional distribution of non-neuronal cell types only exhibits positive correlations with the hierarchy level.
Type: single-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Finally, the regional distribution of non-neuronal cell types only exhibits positive correlations with the hierarchy level.

Q: What are the laminar and regional distribution characteristics of the ASC.11 cell type?
A: ASC.11 is predominantly located in layer 6 and is preferentially enriched in the occipital lobe.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Notably, we also identified two groups of ASCs, group 1 (ASC.1 GFAP/ APLNR and ASC.2 MSX2/TFAP2C) and group 2 (ASC.11 ADGRV1/GRAMD2), which were predominantly located in L1 and L6, respectively (Figures 6B and S6B).
  - Evidence 2: Among ASC cell types, ASC.10, ASC.11, ASC.13, and ASC.16 were preferentially enriched in the occipital lobe.

Q: What correlation does the first principal component of PV/SST cell-type composition show with hierarchy, and which major cell class has more cell types negatively correlated with hierarchy?
A: The first principal component shows a positive correlation with hierarchical levels; glutamatergic cell types have a much higher number of negatively correlated cell types than GABAergic cell types.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: The first principal component (PC1) of various regions exhibited a positive correlation with the hierarchical levels of both sensory systems (Figure 5J), suggesting that the PV and SST interneurons with distinct soma- and dendritic-targeting properties are tightly regulated to support the functions of various cortical regions.
  - Evidence 2: Third, the number of glutamatergic cell types with negative correlation with hierarchy is much higher than that of GABAergic cell types.

Q: Which non-neuronal cell types are distributed in a gradient along the A-P axis, and what are their laminar preferences?
A: ASC, MG, and OLG cell types show a gradient along the anterior-posterior axis; laminar preferences include EC and VLMC predominantly in L1, OLGs enriched in L6, and ASCs dispersed across layers.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Systematic evaluation of the regional distribution of non-neuronal cell types showed that ASC, MG, and OLG cell types were distributed in a gradient along the A-P axis (Figure 6E; Table S5).
  - Evidence 2: For examples, EC and VLMC cell types were predominantly distributed in L1, OLG cells were enriched in L6, and ASCs were dispersed throughout cortical layers (Figure 6A).

Q: What are the A-P axis distribution patterns of the two OLG groups, and how does non-neuronal cell distribution overall correlate with hierarchy?
A: Group 1 OLGs are localized to medial regions and group 2 OLGs to posterior regions along the A-P axis; overall, the regional distribution of non-neuronal cell types only shows positive correlations with hierarchy.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Among OLG cell types, we identified two groups based on their global distribution: group 1 cells (OLG.3 ANLN/CNDP1, OLG.5 PLP1/ERMN, OLG.7 ANLN/MOBP, and OLG.8 PLP1/ OPALIN) and group 2 cells (OLG.6 PLP1/MOG and OLG.11 ANLN/MOG) were localized to medial and posterior regions along the A-P axis, respectively (Figure 6E).
  - Evidence 2: Finally, the regional distribution of non-neuronal cell types only exhibits positive correlations with the hierarchy level.

Q: How many primate-specific L4 glutamatergic cell types were discovered, and what key genes do they highly express?
A: Six primate-specific L4 glutamatergic cell types were found in macaques and humans but not mice; they highly express genes including FOXP2, EPHA3, and DCC.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Remarkably, a pairwise comparison of the transcriptome profiles of cell types uncovered six glutamatergic L4 cell types that were present in the PFC, F1, and V1 regions of macaques and humans but not in mice (Figures 7A–7C, marked in red).
  - Evidence 2: In addition, we found that 53 out of the 226 genes, including FOXP2, EPHA3, and DCC, showed much higher expression levels in the L4/5 IT subclass neurons of humans and macaques than in those of mice (Figure 7G).

Q: How does the correlation with hierarchy differ between the PV/SST density ratio and the non-neuronal cell distribution?
A: The PV/SST density ratio shows no significant correlation with hierarchy, whereas the regional distribution of non-neuronal cells only exhibits positive correlations.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: However, PV and SST density ratio in macaque did not show any significant correlation with the hierarchical levels in either sensory system (Figure S5L).
  - Evidence 2: Finally, the regional distribution of non-neuronal cell types only exhibits positive correlations with the hierarchy level.

Q: Which group of ASCs is enriched in layer 6, and how does their abundance compare between V1 and the prefrontal cortex?
A: Group 2 ASCs (including ASC.11) are predominantly located in layer 6; these L6-enriched ASCs are more abundant in V1 than in the prefrontal cortex.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Notably, we also identified two groups of ASCs, group 1 (ASC.1 GFAP/ APLNR and ASC.2 MSX2/TFAP2C) and group 2 (ASC.11 ADGRV1/GRAMD2), which were predominantly located in L1 and L6, respectively (Figures 6B and S6B).
  - Evidence 2: In addition to the laminar preference, we also observed that L6-enriched ASCs were more abundant in V1 than in prefrontal cortex (PFC) (Figure 6B).

Q: What cell type novelty was found in primates, and how does hierarchical organization in primate brains compare to mice?
A: Six L4 glutamatergic cell types are present in macaques and humans but absent in mice; the primate brain is more hierarchically organized than the mouse brain.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Remarkably, a pairwise comparison of the transcriptome profiles of cell types uncovered six glutamatergic L4 cell types that were present in the PFC, F1, and V1 regions of macaques and humans but not in mice (Figures 7A–7C, marked in red).
  - Evidence 2: The primate brain is more hierarchically organized than the mouse brain.
