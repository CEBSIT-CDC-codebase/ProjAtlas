Q: What percentage of SPNs have spinal cord collaterals, and which subtype forms the spino-mesencephalic tract?

Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Besides the supraspinal projections, we found that 18% of SPNs have collaterals in the spinal cord, categorized as contralateral (mainly in subtypes S9, S16, and S17) or ipsilateral (mainly in subtypes S2, S10, and S11), terminating in the dorsal horn (Figures S6E-S6I).
  - Evidence 2: Interestingly, a distinct subtype (S19) exhibited dedicated innervation of the midbrain, forming the spino-mesencephalic tract (Figures 1G, 1P, and S6B), with most neurons co-innervating the SCm, inferior colliculus (IC), and PAG, pointing to a possible direct pathway for recruiting the circuit for defensive behavior.

Q: Does the number of innervated cortical areas differ between first-order and higher-order thalamic nuclei, and what determines the cortical-layer preference of thalamic relay neurons?
A: Yes, neurons in higher-order thalamic nuclei innervate more cortical areas; cortical-layer preference depends on both the thalamic soma location and the cortical target.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: The number of cortical areas innervated by neurons in higher-order thalamic nuclei was more than that by neurons in first-order thalamic nuclei (Figures S9C and S9D).
  - Evidence 2: Thus, the cortical-layer preference of thalamic relay neurons depends on both their thalamic soma location and their cortical targets, implicating their differential roles in engaging and regulating local cortical circuits.

Q: What are the three projection patterns of PB-projecting SPNs, and what thalamic preferences do subtypes S4 and S10 exhibit?
A: PB-projecting SPNs show ipsilateral-only (S14), contralateral-only (S17), and bilateral (S15) patterns; S4 prefers first-order thalamic nuclei (VP, PoT) while S10 prefers higher-order thalamic nuclei (Eth, PO).
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: For the 8 non-diencephalon-projecting subtypes, we have identified three distinct projection patterns for PB-projecting SPNs, including ipsilateral-only (S14), contralateral-only (S17), and bilateral (S15) patterns (Figures 1G and 1N).
  - Evidence 2: By contrast, subtypes S4 and S10 exhibited preference for first-order (ventral-posterior complex of the thalamus [VP], posterior triangular thalamic nucleus [PoT]) and higher-order (ethmoid nucleus of the thalamus [Eth], PO) thalamus, respectively (Figures 1G, 1I, and 1J).

Q: In how many SPN subtypes were dendritic and axonal arbor lengths correlated, and what are the two broad projection groups of SPNs?
A: Correlations were found in 11 out of 19 subtypes; SPNs are broadly grouped into 11 diencephalon-projecting and 8 non-diencephalon-projecting subtypes.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: Importantly, dendritic and axonal arbor length showed correlations in 11 out of 19 subtypes (p < 0.05, Pearson correlation) (Figures S7B and S7C), indicating a coordinated development of dendritic and axonal arbors.
  - Evidence 2: Based on the innervation strengths (see STAR Methods), SPNs were further classified into 11 diencephalon-projecting subtypes (S1-S11) and 8 non-diencephalon-projecting subtypes (S12-S19) (Figures 1G, 1J, 1N, 1P, and S5A-S5D; Video S1).

Q: What type of neurons were reconstructed besides spinal projection neurons, and which thalamic subtypes target higher-order cortices via the third route?
A: Central relay neurons that receive direct spinal inputs were also reconstructed; subtypes TH_4 and TH_5 channel information to higher-order cortices (AI, PL, ORB) via the third route.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: We also reconstructed the brain-wide axonal projections of central relay neurons that receive direct spinal inputs at the single-cell resolution.
  - Evidence 2: Neurons in subtypes TH_4 and TH_5 channeled information to higher-order cortices, including the agranular insular area (AI), prelimbic area (PL), and orbital area (ORB), via the third route (Figures 3E-3G).

Q: How do ipsilateral PB relay neurons differ from contralateral ones in their downstream innervation, and what could this explain?

Type: multi-evidence

  - Evidence 2: "Thus, ipsilateral and contralateral PB relay neurons exhibited different projection patterns, which could account for hemispheric differences in sensory signal processing via the PB relay pathway."

Q: What proportion of central relay neurons are bilateral, and what does this imply?

Q: How is the maximum depth of an axonal edge assigned, and what defines the axonal trunk based on this depth?
A: Maximum depth is assigned by starting with terminal edges at depth 0 and increasing the parent edge depth when its order is higher than the child's; the axonal trunk then consists of edges whose maximum depth exceeds half the neuron's maximum depth or whose maximum path from branch point to terminals exceeds one-tenth of the overall maximum path.
Type: multi-evidence
Source:
  - Evidence 1: "To determine the maximum depth of each edge, we set the terminal edge as depth 0 and the depth of its parent edge was defined according to its relationship with the children edges with maximum depth."
  - Evidence 2: "The axonal trunk was defined as the edges with the maximum depth greater than half of maximum depth of the entire neuron, or that with the maximum path from the branch point to all downstream terminal points is longer than one-tenth of the maximum path from root to all the terminals."
